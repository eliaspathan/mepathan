---\ntitle: How to Build a Training Analytics Dashboard in Power BI\ndescription:Build a Power BI training analytics dashboard with participant counts, session-level training hours, engagement metrics, trends and course rankings.\npubDate: 2026-09-10\nlabel: Power BI\n---\n\n# <p>Training data often starts as a simple attendance list, but Power BI can turn it into a useful dashboard that shows participation, engagement, training hours, popular courses, and department trends.</p><p>The key is to model the data around training sessions and attendees rather than simply counting rows.</p><h2>Start with the right grain</h2><p>One training session may have many attendees. If the attendance table contains one row per attendee, summing the training duration directly will multiply the hours by the number of participants.</p><p>To calculate total delivered training hours, count each training session only once.</p><h2>Recommended fields</h2><p>A useful attendance dataset might include Training ID, Training Name, Training Date, Start Time, End Time, Duration, Participant ID, Department, and Trainer.</p><p>If the same course is delivered on different dates, each delivery should have its own Training ID.</p><h2>Total participants</h2><pre><code>Total Participants =
DISTINCTCOUNT(Attendance[ParticipantID])</code></pre><p>This counts unique participants within the current report filter context.</p><h2>Total training sessions</h2><pre><code>Total Training Sessions =
DISTINCTCOUNT(Attendance[TrainingID])</code></pre><h2>Total delivered hours</h2><p>If duration is repeated on every attendee row, calculate the duration once for each Training ID.</p><pre><code>Total Training Hours =
SUMX(
    VALUES(Attendance[TrainingID]),
    CALCULATE(MAX(Attendance[DurationHours]))
)</code></pre><p>This prevents a one-hour session with 30 attendees from incorrectly appearing as 30 delivered hours.</p><h2>Average attendance per session</h2><pre><code>Average Attendance per Training =
DIVIDE(
    COUNTROWS(Attendance),
    [Total Training Sessions]
)</code></pre><h2>Useful visuals</h2><p>Start with KPI cards for Total Participants, Total Training Sessions, Total Training Hours, and Average Attendance.</p><p>Then add a monthly attendance trend, a bar chart for attendance by department, a ranking of training courses, and a trainer-level view if trainer data is available.</p><h2>Find the most popular training</h2><p>A simple bar chart with Training Name on the axis and attendee count as the value quickly shows which courses attract the most people.</p><p>You can also create a Top N selector if users need to switch between the top 5, top 10, or another number.</p><h2>Measure engagement, not only attendance</h2><p>High attendance does not always mean high engagement. Useful additional metrics can include repeat participation, completion rates, training hours per employee, and the percentage of employees who attended at least one session.</p><h2>Use a date table</h2><p>Create a dedicated date table and connect it to Training Date. This makes month, quarter, year, and rolling-period analysis easier and more consistent.</p><h2>Normalize training names</h2><p>Course names often change slightly over time. For example, extra spaces or different abbreviations can split one course into several categories. A mapping table can standardize course names without changing the original source data.</p><h2>Final thoughts</h2><p>A useful training dashboard should answer a few simple questions: How many people are participating? Which courses are most popular? How many training hours are being delivered? Which departments are engaged? And how is participation changing over time?</p><p>Build the model around those questions, keep session-level and attendee-level calculations separate, and the dashboard will be far more reliable.</p>\
